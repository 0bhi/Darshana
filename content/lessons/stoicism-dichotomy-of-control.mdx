Epictetus opens the *Enchiridion* with a distinction that has been repeated for nearly two thousand years: some things are up to us, and some are not. Up to us are our judgments, impulses, desires, and aversions. Not up to us are our bodies, reputations, possessions, and the actions of others.

The distinction is simple. The practice is not.

## The argument

Stoic ethics rests on a claim about what makes a life go well. The only genuine good is virtue, and the only genuine evil is vice. Everything else - health, wealth, status, even life itself - is a "preferred indifferent." It is reasonable to prefer health to sickness, but health is not what makes a life good, because it can be lost and because it is not fully within our control.

If your happiness depends on something you cannot control, you have placed your well-being in unstable hands. If it depends on your own judgments and choices, you have located it where you have some authority.

<ThoughtExperiment title="The archer">

A Stoic image compares life to archery. The archer can control her stance, aim, and release. She cannot control the wind, the movement of the target, or the quality of the bow.

The good archer does everything within her power and accepts the outcome without confusion. Her skill is displayed in the attempt, not in the result.

</ThoughtExperiment>

## What is up to us?

The Stoics do not claim that we can control our circumstances or even our initial emotional reactions. They claim that we can control our assent: whether we endorse a judgment, whether we act on an impulse, whether we allow a impression to carry us away.

Epictetus distinguishes impressions from judgments. An impression is the initial appearance that something is terrible. A judgment is the endorsement of that appearance. The practice is to pause between them.

This is why Stoicism is often described as cognitive therapy before cognitive therapy. The goal is not to feel nothing. It is to examine the judgments that make suffering worse than it needs to be.

## Acceptance is not passivity

A common caricature says that Stoics are emotionless and passive. The caricature is false.

Stoics are committed to justice, courage, and care for others. Marcus Aurelius governed an empire; Seneca advised a emperor; Epictetus taught freed slaves and aristocrats. Their acceptance concerns outcomes, not effort. You should try to change what can be changed. What you should not do is tie your peace to the result.

<Caveat title="The control fallacy">

The dichotomy of control can be misused. Not everything outside your mind is beyond influence. You can influence your health, your relationships, and your community. The Stoic point is that influence is not control. You can act well and still fail.

</Caveat>

## Objections

### Is virtue really enough?

The Stoic claim that virtue alone is sufficient for happiness can seem implausible. Severe illness, bereavement, and injustice cause real suffering. The Stoic response is that these are not evils in the strict sense, but they are still dispreferred. Whether this is a profound insight or a denial of human vulnerability is a central dispute.

### Does the dichotomy blame victims?

If suffering depends on judgment, then victims of injustice may be told that their suffering is their own fault. This is a serious misreading. Stoicism does not deny that external events are bad; it denies that they are the ultimate determinants of a good life. The practice is not a substitute for justice.

### Is emotional detachment desirable?

Stoics aim for *apatheia*, freedom from destructive passions, not the elimination of all feeling. They value joy, cheerfulness, and affection. But critics argue that the ideal of equanimity can suppress legitimate grief and anger.

## Going deeper

### The Stoic cosmos

Ancient Stoicism was a complete philosophical system, not only a set of coping techniques. It included physics and logic as well as ethics. The cosmos was understood as a rational, providential order. The dichotomy of control makes sense within that worldview: if the universe is governed by reason, then aligning one's judgments with reason is the way to live well.

Modern Stoicism often strips away the metaphysics. That can make it more accessible, but it also raises the question of whether the ethics can stand without the cosmology.

### Comparison with Buddhism

Stoicism and Buddhism both emphasise the role of desire and judgment in suffering, and both recommend practices of attention. But they differ on metaphysics, on the status of the self, and on the goal. Stoics seek to live according to nature and reason; Buddhists seek liberation from conditioned existence.

## Practice

The dichotomy of control is best learned through a daily exercise. Each morning, list what you are anxious about. Mark each item as up to you, partly up to you, or not up to you. For the first, plan an action. For the second, plan an action and release the outcome. For the third, practise acknowledgment.

This is not a cure for anxiety. It is a way of preventing anxiety from colonising more of your life than it needs to.

## What to carry away

The Stoic dichotomy of control is a discipline of attention. It asks you to locate your well-being in your own judgments and choices rather than in outcomes you cannot guarantee. The goal is not indifference but freedom: the freedom to act well without being destroyed by failure.
