The problem of evil is as old as philosophy of religion, and it remains one of the most powerful challenges to belief in a good God. The challenge is not simply that suffering exists. It is that suffering seems difficult to reconcile with the traditional attributes of God: omnipotence, omniscience, and omnibenevolence.

## The logical problem

The logical problem claims that the existence of evil is logically incompatible with the existence of God. J. L. Mackie formulated it as a set of inconsistent propositions:

1. God is omnipotent.
2. God is omniscient.
3. God is omnibenevolent.
4. Evil exists.

If God is omnipotent, he could prevent evil. If he is omniscient, he knows about it. If he is omnibenevolent, he would want to prevent it. If evil exists, something must be wrong with at least one of the first three claims.

The argument is valid only if there is no possible reason for God to permit evil. The free will defence attempts to provide one.

## The free will defence

The free will defence argues that a world with significant freedom is better than a world without it, and that significant freedom requires the possibility of moral evil. God could not create free creatures and guarantee that they never choose wrongly without removing the freedom that makes them valuable.

This response has limits. It addresses moral evil, the evil caused by human choices. It is less effective against natural evil: earthquakes, diseases, and the suffering of animals. Some defenders extend the argument to the choices of non-human agents, but this is speculative.

<ThoughtExperiment title="The locked room">

A parent can keep a child in a locked room, safe from every harm. The child will never be injured, but she will also never learn, explore, or grow. Is the locked room a loving environment?

The free will defence makes a similar claim about God and human freedom. A world without the possibility of wrongdoing might be safer, but it would also lack the goods that depend on genuine agency.

</ThoughtExperiment>

## The evidential problem

Even if the logical problem fails, the evidential problem remains. The amount and distribution of suffering seem excessive for any plausible divine purpose. A fawn trapped in a forest fire, a child dying of bone cancer, the slow erosion of a mind by dementia - these are not obviously necessary for freedom or moral growth.

The evidential argument does not claim a contradiction. It claims that the existence of such suffering makes belief in God unreasonable, or at least less reasonable than it would otherwise be.

## Responses

### Soul-making

John Hick's soul-making theodicy argues that suffering is justified as a condition for spiritual growth. A world with challenges is necessary for the development of virtues such as courage, compassion, and patience. The goal is not comfort but character.

The objection is that the amount of suffering seems disproportionate. Some suffering destroys people rather than refining them. And the distribution is deeply unequal: some people endure far more than any plausible soul-making benefit could require.

### Skeptical theism

Skeptical theism argues that human beings cannot know God's reasons. If God's mind is vastly greater than ours, we should not expect to see the justification for every instance of suffering. The absence of an apparent reason is not evidence that there is no reason.

The objection is that this response risks undermining all moral judgment. If we cannot know God's reasons, how can we object to any suffering? The skeptical theist must distinguish between epistemic humility and moral paralysis.

### The cross and protest

Some Christian responses locate God's answer to evil not in explanation but in participation. God enters suffering in the crucifixion and shares in human anguish. This is not a theodicy in the sense of a justification; it is a response of solidarity.

Other traditions emphasise protest. The Book of Job ends with God answering Job not with an explanation but with a display of power and mystery. Job's demand for justice is not answered on its own terms.

<Caveat title="The difference between explanation and justification">

A theodicy attempts to justify God's permission of evil. A defence attempts only to show that the existence of evil is not logically incompatible with God. The distinction matters: showing that evil is possible under God's rule is not the same as showing that this much evil is acceptable.

</Caveat>

## Non-theistic perspectives

The problem of evil is not only a problem for theists. Buddhists explain suffering through craving and ignorance, but they must still address why the causal order produces so much unnecessary pain. Stoics and existentialists offer practices for living with suffering rather than explanations of its ultimate purpose. Camus refuses the demand for explanation altogether.

## What it changes

The problem of evil is a test case for the coherence of theism and for the limits of philosophical explanation. It also raises a moral question that no argument can avoid: what should we do about suffering? Explaining evil is not the same as responding to it.

## What to carry away

The logical problem of evil challenges the consistency of traditional divine attributes. The evidential problem challenges the reasonableness of belief in light of the amount and distribution of suffering. The free will defence, soul-making, and skeptical theism each address part of the challenge, but none commands universal assent. The problem remains open.
