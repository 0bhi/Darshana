Death is the horizon of every human life. It is also one of the strangest objects of philosophical reflection, because it is not an experience anyone can report. We can witness the death of others, but our own death is something we can only imagine from the outside.

## Epicurus: death is nothing to us

Epicurus offers a famously deflationary argument. Death, he says, is nothing to us. While we exist, death is not present. When death is present, we do not exist. There is no moment at which we experience death as bad.

The argument depends on the assumption that only experiences can be good or bad for a person. If death is the absence of experience, it cannot be bad. At most, we can fear the process of dying, but not death itself.

<PullQuote source="Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus (trans. C. D. Yonge)">

Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not.

</PullQuote>

## Nagel: death as deprivation

Thomas Nagel rejects Epicurus's conclusion. Death can be bad for a person even if it is not experienced. It deprives the person of the goods that continued life would have brought: experiences, relationships, projects, and achievements.

Nagel's account is comparative. Death is bad because it makes the person's life worse than it would have been. The person who dies young is deprived of more than the person who dies old, other things equal. This matches many of our intuitions.

The account is not without problems. If death is bad because it deprives us of future goods, is it also bad for a person who would have lived a miserable life? Nagel says no; death is bad only when the life would have been worth living.

## The symmetry argument

Lucretius offers a symmetry argument. Before we were born, we did not exist, and we do not regret the time before our birth. Why should we regret the time after our death?

The argument has been criticised on several grounds. Some philosophers argue that our attitudes toward the past and the future are not symmetric because the future is open and the past is fixed. Others argue that the comparison is flawed because the pre-natal absence is not a loss of a life that was already underway.

<ThoughtExperiment title="The immortal life">

Bernard Williams asks us to imagine a life that never ends. If we could live forever, would we want to? Williams argues that an immortal life would eventually become meaningless. Our projects and attachments are shaped by finitude. A life without end would lose its urgency and eventually its value.

Whether Williams is right is contested. Some philosophers argue that an endless life could include endless novelty and growth. Others argue that the value of life is not exhausted by its projects.

</ThoughtExperiment>

## Mortality and meaning

If death is inevitable, does it make life meaningless? There are several possible responses.

**The pessimistic response**: death renders all achievements temporary, and therefore meaningless. This is the view Schopenhauer sometimes suggests and that Nietzsche diagnoses as nihilism.

**The existential response**: death is what makes life urgent and valuable. Because we have limited time, our choices matter. Camus's absurd hero lives with the knowledge of death and still affirms life.

**The Buddhist response**: death is a reminder of impermanence. Clinging to permanence produces suffering. The practice is to accept impermanence and to live with awareness.

**The Epicurean response**: death is nothing to us. We should focus on the quality of the present life and cultivate tranquillity.

<Caveat title="No easy consolation">

The philosophical literature on death does not yield a single answer. The arguments are meant to clarify the question, not to provide comfort. Anyone who claims to have settled the meaning of death is probably oversimplifying.

</Caveat>

## Heidegger and being-toward-death

Martin Heidegger argues that death is the most ownmost possibility of Dasein. It is not a general event that happens to others; it is something each person must face. Authentic existence involves anticipating death and allowing it to shape one's life.

Heidegger's account is dense and controversial, but its central insight is widely shared: awareness of death can change how we live. It can strip away trivial concerns and clarify what matters.

## Objections

### Is death bad for the dead?

If the dead do not exist, can they be harmed? Nagel's deprivation account says yes, but the metaphysics is disputed. Some philosophers argue that death is bad only in relation to the living who anticipate it.

### Is immortality desirable?

The question of whether death is bad depends partly on whether life is good. If life contains more suffering than joy, death may be a release. The value of death is not independent of the value of life.

### Can meaning survive death?

Many people find meaning in projects that outlast them: children, works of art, institutions, and contributions to knowledge. Others find meaning in the present, without concern for legacy. The question is not whether meaning survives death but what kind of meaning matters to us.

## Living with the question

Death is not only a theoretical problem. It is a practical horizon. The Stoics recommend *memento mori*, the remembrance of death, not as morbidity but as a way to prioritize. If your time is limited, what deserves your attention?

The question can be paralyzing or clarifying. The difference often lies in whether you face it with denial or with honesty.

## What to carry away

Epicurus argues that death is nothing to us; Nagel argues that it is bad because it deprives us of goods. The symmetry argument challenges our attitudes toward past and future non-existence. Mortality can make life seem meaningless or precious, depending on the framework. The question remains open, but it is one worth living with.
