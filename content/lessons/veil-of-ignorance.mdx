Suppose you had to design the basic institutions of a society without knowing who you would be in it. You would not know your class, race, gender, talents, health, or conception of the good. What principles would you choose?

John Rawls calls this thought experiment the original position, and the ignorance condition the veil of ignorance. The point is not that anyone has actually made such a choice. It is that the principles we would choose under these conditions give us a standard of justice.

## The original position

The original position is a hypothetical situation of fair agreement. Parties are rational and mutually disinterested: they care about their own interests but not about others' interests as such. They are not envious, and they are not self-sacrificing.

Because they do not know their place in society, they cannot choose principles that favour a particular group. They must choose principles that are acceptable whatever position they end up in. Rawls argues that this procedure models the idea that society is a fair system of cooperation among free and equal persons.

<ThoughtExperiment title="Choosing the rules">

You are behind the veil. You know that society will have a distribution of income, wealth, and opportunity, but you do not know whether you will be rich or poor, healthy or ill, talented or ordinary.

Would you choose a system with extreme inequalities and no safety net? If you turned out to be at the bottom, you would suffer greatly. Would you choose a system that maximises the position of the worst off, even if it limits the gains of the most advantaged?

Rawls argues that rational parties would choose the latter.

</ThoughtExperiment>

## The two principles

Rawls derives two principles of justice.

1. **The liberty principle**: each person has an equal right to the most extensive basic liberties compatible with similar liberties for others.
2. **The difference principle**: social and economic inequalities are just only if they are attached to positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity, and only if they are to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society.

The first principle has priority over the second. Liberty cannot be traded for economic gain. The second principle combines fair equality of opportunity with the difference principle.

## Why the difference principle?

The difference principle is the most distinctive and controversial part of Rawls's theory. It permits inequalities, but only when they improve the position of the worst off. If an inequality makes the rich richer without benefiting the poor, it is unjust.

The reasoning behind it is that natural talents and social starting points are arbitrary from a moral point of view. No one deserves their native intelligence, their family, or their country of birth. The distribution of these advantages should not determine the distribution of life prospects unless the arrangement benefits everyone.

## The social contract tradition

Rawls is not the first to use a contract device. Hobbes imagines a state of nature in which life is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short. The social contract is a way to escape that condition. Locke imagines a state of nature governed by natural law and argues that government exists to protect property and liberty.

Rawls's contract is different. It is not a historical event or a justification of a particular sovereign. It is a thought experiment for testing principles of justice. The agreement is hypothetical, and the parties are not choosing a ruler but a basic structure.

<Caveat title="The veil is not a real ignorance">

The veil of ignorance is a device of representation. It does not imply that real people should ignore their identities or that knowledge of one's social position is a moral failing. Its purpose is to model impartiality.

</Caveat>

## Objections

### Nozick and entitlement

Robert Nozick argues that justice is historical, not patterned. If holdings are acquired justly and transferred justly, the resulting distribution is just, regardless of whether it benefits the least advantaged. The difference principle, on this view, ignores the history of acquisition and treats people as means to a pattern.

### Communitarian critique

Communitarians argue that Rawls's parties are abstract individuals without attachments. Real people are constituted by their communities, traditions, and commitments. Choosing principles behind a veil may produce a theory that is blind to the goods that make life meaningful.

### The disability critique

Some critics argue that the veil of ignorance assumes a society of independent, productive adults. It may not adequately represent the interests of people with severe disabilities or those who depend on care. Rawlsian theory has been extended to address these concerns, but the debate continues.

### Is the difference principle too demanding?

If the least advantaged must benefit from every inequality, the principle may require extensive redistribution. Defenders argue that it is a standard of justice, not a policy manual, and that it can be implemented in different ways.

## Living with the question

The veil of ignorance is a practical tool for moral imagination. When you are designing a policy, a workplace rule, or a family arrangement, ask whether you would accept it if you did not know which position you would occupy.

The question does not settle every dispute. But it can expose self-serving assumptions and make room for a more impartial conversation.

## What to carry away

Rawls's veil of ignorance models fair agreement. It yields two principles: equal basic liberties and the difference principle. The theory is a powerful alternative to utilitarianism and libertarianism, though it faces serious objections. Its central insight is that justice must be justified to everyone, including those who fare worst.
