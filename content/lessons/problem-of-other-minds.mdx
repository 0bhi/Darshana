You know your own pain from the inside. You do not infer it from your behaviour; you simply have it. With other people, the situation is reversed. You see the wince, hear the cry, and read the report. You never have their pain.

This asymmetry is the problem of other minds. It is not the question of whether other people are usually honest. It is the question of what justifies the belief that there is anything it is like to be them at all.

## The problem

The sceptical challenge can be stated in three steps.

1. Your knowledge of your own mental states is direct and first-personal.
2. Your knowledge of other people is limited to their bodies, behaviour, and words.
3. Nothing observable logically entails the presence of experience.

If the third step is right, then the existence of other minds is an inference rather than an observation. And if the inference is weak, scepticism about other minds becomes difficult to refute.

<ThoughtExperiment title="The perfect actor">

Suppose you meet someone who behaves exactly as a person in pain would behave: she cries out, clutches her hand, avoids using it, and says she is in agony. But suppose, unknown to you, she is a highly sophisticated machine with no inner life.

If her behaviour is indistinguishable from a pained person's, what evidence could settle the question? If no evidence could settle it, is the belief in her pain justified?

</ThoughtExperiment>

## Why it matters

The problem is not merely academic. It shapes how we treat animals, infants, people with disorders of consciousness, and artificial systems. If we require behavioural evidence, some beings may qualify; if we require similarity to human brains, others may not.

It also matters ethically. Much of morality depends on the assumption that others can suffer and flourish. A view that treats other minds as unverifiable may not license cruelty, but it can weaken the grounds for caring about others.

## Responses

### The argument from analogy

The classic response reasons from one's own case. I know that my pain causes me to wince. Other people wince. Therefore, by analogy, they probably experience pain.

The argument has obvious weaknesses. It generalises from a single case, and it assumes that similar behaviour has similar causes. It also cannot rule out the possibility that others are complex automata.

### Inference to the best explanation

A stronger response treats other minds as a theoretical posit. The best explanation of human behaviour appeals to beliefs, desires, and experiences. We cannot observe electrons either, but we posit them because they explain the data.

The objection is that mental states are not needed to explain behaviour in the way electrons are needed to explain tracks in a cloud chamber. A purely physical explanation might suffice.

### Direct perception

Some philosophers argue that we perceive other minds in behaviour, not behind it. When you see a face contorted in pain, you see the pain itself, not a mere sign of it. On this view, the problem is generated by a false picture of experience as a private inner theatre.

This response is associated with Wittgenstein and with phenomenologists. Its strength is that it captures how we ordinarily relate to others. Its weakness is that it can seem to deny the obvious fact that experience is private.

### Intersubjectivity and testimony

Another response shifts the burden. We are trained into a shared world of expressions, language, and norms. Knowledge of other minds is not built from isolated inferences; it is sustained by practices of testimony and mutual recognition. Scepticism about other minds may be a theoretical artefact rather than a live practical possibility.

<Caveat title="Scepticism is not refuted by convenience">

It is tempting to say that we must believe in other minds to get on with life. That is true, but it is not a philosophical refutation of scepticism. The question is whether the belief is justified, not whether it is useful.

</Caveat>

## Going deeper

### The problem of animal minds

If we require similarity to human behaviour and brains, many animals may be excluded. Yet comparative psychology provides evidence of pain behaviour, learning, and social cognition. The philosophical question is how much weight to give to behavioural and neurological evidence when direct access is impossible.

### Artificial minds

Large language models can produce text that sounds as if it understands. Does that provide evidence of understanding or experience? The Chinese room argument suggests that symbol manipulation is not sufficient for understanding. But the problem of other minds is broader: even if a system understood, we would face the same epistemic barrier.

### The Buddhist analysis

Buddhist philosophy does not begin with a private inner self that must be inferred in others. It analyses experience as a stream of conditioned events and emphasises the constructed nature of the self-other boundary. This does not dissolve the epistemic problem, but it changes the terms in which it is posed.

## Living with the question

The problem of other minds can make us more careful and more generous. More careful, because we should not assume that our interpretation of another's behaviour is transparent. More generous, because the fact that experience is not directly accessible is a reason to listen to testimony rather than dismiss it.

It can also deepen loneliness. If no one can directly know your experience, then even the closest relationship involves interpretation. Whether that is a tragedy or simply the condition of finite beings is a question worth considering.

## What to carry away

The problem of other minds arises from the privacy of experience. The argument from analogy is the most natural response, but it is not decisive. Inferential, perceptual, and intersubjective accounts each capture something important. The problem is not solved, but it is clarified by distinguishing the epistemic question from the ethical one.
