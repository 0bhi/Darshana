When the Buddha is asked whether there is a self, he is famously silent. When he is asked whether there is no self, he is also silent. The silence is not evasion. It signals that the question, as posed, may be the wrong one.

The teaching of *anatta*, often translated as not-self, is an analysis of what we find when we look for the self. It is not a denial that persons exist. It is a denial that persons exist as permanent, independent essences.

## The five aggregates

Buddhist analysis divides the person into five aggregates:

1. **Form**: the body and material elements.
2. **Feeling**: pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral sensations.
3. **Perception**: recognition and labelling.
4. **Mental formations**: intentions, habits, emotions, and volitional factors.
5. **Consciousness**: awareness of objects.

The claim is that a person is a collection of these processes, not a sixth thing that owns them. When you look for the self, you find a body, feelings, perceptions, formations, and consciousness. You do not find an extra entity behind them.

<ThoughtExperiment title="The chariot">

King Milinda asks the monk Nagasena who he is. Nagasena replies by asking whether the chariot is the wheels, the axle, the frame, the reins, or the driver. The king says no to each. Nagasena asks whether the chariot is something apart from these parts. The king says no.

The point is not that chariots do not exist. It is that "chariot" is a conventional designation for a collection of parts arranged in a certain way. The same analysis applies to persons.

</ThoughtExperiment>

## The argument from impermanence

The *Anattalakkhana Sutta* argues that each aggregate is impermanent. What is impermanent is unsatisfactory, because it cannot provide lasting security. And what is impermanent and unsatisfactory cannot reasonably be regarded as self.

The argument can be reconstructed:

1. If something were truly self, it would be subject to control and would not cause suffering.
2. The aggregates are impermanent and not fully controllable.
3. Therefore, none of the aggregates is self.
4. Nothing else is a candidate for self.
5. Therefore, there is no self in the relevant sense.

This is an argument about what the self would have to be like, not a direct proof that no self exists. It targets views that identify the self with the body, feelings, perception, formations, or consciousness.

## What is denied?

Anatta denies *svabhava*: intrinsic, independent essence. It does not deny:

- That persons exist as conventional designations.
- That experiences are continuous and causally connected.
- That actions have consequences.
- That liberation is possible.

The distinction between conventional truth and ultimate truth is central. Conventionally, we say that a person is born, acts, suffers, and dies. Ultimately, these are processes without a permanent owner. Both levels are true in their own domains.

<Caveat title="Not nihilism">

It is tempting to conclude that if there is no self, then nothing matters. This is precisely the view the Buddha rejects. The absence of a fixed self does not erase suffering, relationship, or ethical responsibility. It changes the understanding of what is suffering and who is liberated.

</Caveat>

## Responsibility without a self

If there is no self, who acts and who receives the results of action? The Buddhist answer is causal continuity. A person is a stream of conditioned events. Actions shape future events in the same stream, and the process is conventionally called "the same person."

This is not fundamentally different from the way we hold a river responsible for its downstream effects, or a company responsible for its actions. The absence of a permanent essence does not eliminate causal responsibility.

## Going deeper

### Rebirth without a self

The doctrine of rebirth without a self is one of the hardest to understand. If there is no soul that transmigrates, what is reborn? The traditional answer is that rebirth is the continuation of a causal process, not the transfer of a substance. The flame of one candle lights another; it is not the same flame, but it is not unrelated.

### Nagarjuna and emptiness

Nagarjuna radicalises the analysis. All phenomena are empty of intrinsic nature because they arise dependently. Emptiness is not nothingness; it is the absence of independent existence. This makes anatta a special case of a broader metaphysical claim.

### Hume and the bundle

Hume's bundle theory is a striking Western parallel. Introspection reveals only perceptions, never a simple self. Hume famously confessed that he could not find a self when he looked. The similarity with Buddhist analysis is real, though the soteriological context is different.

## Living with the question

Anatta is not only a theory. It is a practice of observing experience without identifying with it. When a feeling arises, you can notice it as a feeling rather than as "my pain." When a thought arises, you can notice it as a thought rather than as "who I am."

The aim is not to make the self disappear. It is to loosen the grip of the illusion that there is a fixed self that must be protected, defended, and satisfied.

## What to carry away

Anatta is the teaching that persons lack a permanent, independent essence. It is supported by the analysis of the five aggregates and the argument from impermanence. It is not annihilationism, and it does not eliminate responsibility. Its practical aim is liberation from clinging, not the destruction of the person.
