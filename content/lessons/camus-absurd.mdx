Albert Camus begins *The Myth of Sisyphus* with a question that is not rhetorical: whether life is worth living. He calls it the only truly serious philosophical problem. His answer is not a doctrine of despair. It is an argument for living without consolation.

## The absurd

The absurd, for Camus, is not a property of the world. It is a relation. Human beings demand meaning, clarity, and permanence. The universe, as far as reason can tell, offers none. The absurd is the confrontation between that demand and that silence.

<ThoughtExperiment title="The glass wall">

Imagine you are in a glass booth, speaking to someone on the other side. You can see their lips move, but you cannot hear them. You keep speaking, and they keep answering, but no understanding passes through.

Camus compares the absurd to this broken communication. We call out for meaning; the world does not answer. The absurd is not the silence alone, nor the call alone, but the distance between them.

</ThoughtExperiment>

This is why Camus rejects the idea that the absurd is simply a lack of meaning in the universe. If there were no demand for meaning, there would be no absurd. The absurd is born from the collision.

## The two suicides

Camus distinguishes physical suicide from what he calls philosophical suicide. Physical suicide ends the confrontation by ending the one who demands meaning. Philosophical suicide resolves the tension by adopting a belief that exceeds the evidence, such as a leap of faith.

He rejects both. Suicide, he argues, is a confession that life is not worth living, but it does not answer the question; it avoids it. Philosophical suicide is intellectually dishonest because it smuggles in the consolation it claims to have found.

## Revolt, freedom, and passion

If the absurd cannot be resolved, it can be lived. Camus draws three consequences.

**Revolt.** The absurd person refuses to be reconciled to the silence. She keeps the question open. Revolt is not political revolution, though Camus later develops the connection; it is a stance of lucid refusal.

**Freedom.** Without a predetermined meaning or a promised afterlife, the absurd person is free from the illusions that constrain ordinary life. This freedom is not unlimited; it is the freedom of someone who has accepted the limits of knowledge.

**Passion.** Since the absurd excludes hope for another life, the present life becomes more intense. Camus writes that the absurd person multiplies experiences rather than accumulating them. The point is not quantity for its own sake, but intensity and awareness.

<PullQuote source="Albert Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus">

One must imagine Sisyphus happy.

</PullQuote>

## Sisyphus

The image that gives the essay its title is Sisyphus, condemned to roll a boulder up a hill forever. Each time he nears the top, the boulder rolls back down. The gods intend this as the most terrible punishment: useless, endless labour.

Camus asks us to imagine Sisyphus at the moment the boulder descends. In that moment, Sisyphus is conscious of his fate. He sees the absurd clearly. And yet he can choose to scorn the gods, to find his purpose in the struggle itself. The struggle toward the heights is enough to fill a human heart.

This is not optimism. It is a hard-won lucidity. Sisyphus is not happy because he has found meaning; he is happy because he has stopped demanding that the universe provide one.

## Objections

### Is revolt a form of meaning?

Critics argue that Camus smuggles in a value - revolt - that functions like the meaning he claims to reject. If the absurd forbids consolation, why is revolt not a consolation? Camus's response is that revolt does not resolve the tension; it sustains it.

### Is the absurd a philosophical thesis?

The absurd depends on a claim about the limits of reason and the silence of the universe. That is itself a philosophical position, and it can be challenged. Some philosophers argue that meaning is not something the universe gives or withholds; it is created through human practices and commitments.

### The political turn

Camus's later work, especially *The Rebel*, explores the limits of revolt. He argues that rebellion can become tyranny when it claims absolute truth. This tension between individual lucidity and collective action remains a productive problem in his thought.

<Caveat title="Absurdism is not nihilism">

Nihilism denies that values or meaning exist. Camus does not deny that people create meaning and value; he denies that the universe supplies them. The absurdist can be passionately committed to justice, love, and art. The difference is that she does not expect those commitments to be cosmically guaranteed.

</Caveat>

## Living with the question

Camus's essay is often read as a consolation for people who have lost faith in grand narratives. It is more demanding than that. It asks you to give up the hope that the universe will justify your life, and to live as if the absence of justification were not a reason to stop.

That can be liberating or terrifying, depending on the day. Camus's wager is that it can be both, and that lucidity is worth the cost.

## What to carry away

The absurd is a relation between human demands and a silent world. Camus rejects suicide and philosophical consolation in favour of revolt, freedom, and passion. Whether his response is stable is a live question, but the question he poses is difficult to avoid.
