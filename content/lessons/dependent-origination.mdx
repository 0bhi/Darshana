Dependent origination is the central metaphysical insight of Buddhism. It is stated simply: when this exists, that comes to be; when this ceases, that ceases. Nothing arises without conditions. Nothing exists independently.

The simplicity is deceptive. The doctrine has radical implications for causation, the self, and liberation.

## The twelve links

Buddhist texts present dependent origination as a chain of twelve links:

1. Ignorance conditions volitional formations.
2. Volitional formations condition consciousness.
3. Consciousness conditions name-and-form.
4. Name-and-form conditions the six sense bases.
5. The six sense bases condition contact.
6. Contact conditions feeling.
7. Feeling conditions craving.
8. Craving conditions clinging.
9. Clinging conditions becoming.
10. Becoming conditions birth.
11. Birth conditions aging and death.
12. Aging and death condition suffering.

The chain is not a linear sequence in time. It is a description of how suffering arises through mutually conditioning factors. Each link depends on the others; none is a first cause.

<ThoughtExperiment title="The fire and the fuel">

A fire burns because of fuel, heat, and oxygen. Remove any of these, and the fire goes out. The fire is not a substance that exists independently; it is a process that depends on conditions.

Buddhist texts use this image for the person. The self is not a substance that persists independently; it is a process sustained by conditions. When the conditions change, the process changes.

</ThoughtExperiment>

## Causation without substances

Dependent origination challenges the idea that causation is a relation between independently existing things. If things arise dependently, their causal powers are not intrinsic properties of isolated substances. They are relational.

This is why Buddhist philosophy is often described as a process philosophy. Reality is a network of conditioned events, not a collection of things with fixed essences.

## Nagarjuna and emptiness

Nagarjuna, the second-century Indian philosopher, develops dependent origination into the doctrine of emptiness. To be empty is to lack intrinsic nature, to exist only in dependence on conditions and conceptual designation.

Emptiness is not nothingness. Nagarjuna insists that emptiness itself is empty. It is not a substance, a ground, or a higher reality. It is a way of describing how things are.

This is why the identification of emptiness with dependent origination matters. If things existed by their own nature, they would not depend on conditions. Because they depend on conditions, they are empty. Because they are empty, they can arise and cease.

<Caveat title="Emptiness is not nihilism">

The claim that things are empty does not mean that nothing exists. It means that nothing exists independently. Conventional reality remains real: fire burns, actions have consequences, suffering is real. Emptiness is a description of the mode of existence, not a denial of existence.

</Caveat>

## The two truths

Nagarjuna distinguishes conventional truth from ultimate truth. Conventionally, things exist and function. Ultimately, they are empty of intrinsic nature. The two truths are not two separate worlds; they are two ways of understanding the same reality.

This distinction is central to Madhyamaka ethics. Because conventional reality is not denied, ethical action remains meaningful. Because ultimate reality is empty, clinging to fixed identities and absolutes is undermined.

## Objections

### Is emptiness a view?

If emptiness is the absence of intrinsic nature, is the doctrine of emptiness itself a view with intrinsic nature? Nagarjuna anticipates this objection. He says that emptiness is a medicine, not a doctrine to be clung to. Those who reify emptiness are like a patient who refuses to let go of the cure.

### Does dependent origination explain anything?

Critics argue that saying everything arises dependently is a schema, not an explanation. It does not tell us why any particular thing arises. The Buddhist response is that the doctrine is soteriological: it explains how suffering arises and how it can cease, not why the universe has the structure it does.

### The problem of causation

If nothing has intrinsic nature, how can one thing cause another? Nagarjuna argues that causation is intelligible only conventionally. Ultimately, there are no independent relata to stand in causal relations. This has led to charges of incoherence, but Madhyamaka philosophers have developed sophisticated responses.

## Going deeper

### Comparison with process philosophy

Western process philosophers such as Alfred North Whitehead also reject substance metaphysics in favour of events and relations. The comparison is illuminating, though the metaphysical systems differ significantly.

### Comparison with Advaita

Advaita Vedanta also denies ultimate plurality, but it affirms a single ultimate consciousness, Brahman. Nagarjuna denies any ultimate ground. The debate between these traditions is one of the great philosophical conversations in Indian thought.

## Living with the question

Dependent origination is not only a metaphysical theory. It is a way of seeing experience. When you notice that a feeling depends on conditions, it becomes less solid. When you notice that a self depends on conditions, it becomes less defensive.

The practice is not to deny experience but to see its dependent nature. That seeing is supposed to loosen clinging, and with it, suffering.

## What to carry away

Dependent origination teaches that phenomena arise in dependence on conditions. Nagarjuna identifies this with emptiness: the absence of intrinsic nature. Emptiness is not nihilism, and conventional reality remains meaningful. The doctrine is a powerful alternative to substance metaphysics and a foundation for Buddhist ethics.
