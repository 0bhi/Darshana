Advaita Vedanta is a school of Indian philosophy whose name means "non-dualism." Its central claim is startlingly direct: the self you take to be a finite individual is not other than Brahman, the ultimate reality. The separation is not a fact but a misidentification.

The claim is not that you are a piece of the universe or a part of God. It is that the distinction between the self and the whole is ultimately unreal.

## Atman and Brahman

The Upanishads contain sayings such as "That thou art" and "This self is Brahman." Advaita reads these as identity claims. Atman, the innermost self, is pure consciousness. Brahman, the ground of reality, is also pure consciousness. There cannot be two ultimate consciousnesses, so they must be one.

The apparent individual self - the ego with its memories, desires, and body - is not Atman in its true nature. It is a superimposition. We mistake the self for the body, the mind, or the personality, just as we might mistake a rope for a snake in dim light.

<ThoughtExperiment title="The rope and the snake">

You see a coiled shape in the road at dusk and recoil, thinking it is a snake. When you shine a light, you see it is a rope. The fear was real, but its object was not.

Advaita uses this image for the world of separate things. The plurality is experienced, but it is superimposed on a single reality by ignorance. When knowledge arises, the fear subsides.

</ThoughtExperiment>

## Maya and the status of the world

Maya is often translated as illusion, but the translation can mislead. Advaita does not say that the world is nothing. It says that the world does not have the independent reality we attribute to it.

The doctrine of two levels of truth helps. At the conventional level, the world, persons, and causal relations are real. At the ultimate level, only Brahman is real. The world is real enough for practical purposes, but it is not ultimately separate from Brahman.

This is why Advaita can affirm ethical life while denying ultimate plurality. Suffering is real conventionally; liberation is the recognition that the sufferer and the suffering are not ultimately two.

## Knowledge and liberation

Advaita is a path of knowledge, *jnana*. Liberation is not produced by action, because action operates within the realm of ignorance. It is attained by removing ignorance through inquiry into the nature of the self.

The traditional method involves hearing the teachings, reflecting on them, and sustained meditation. The goal is not to become Brahman but to recognise that one already is.

This has ethical implications. If the self is not separate from others, harm to another is harm to oneself in a deeper sense. Advaita ethics is often expressed through compassion and non-harm, though the school has been criticised for neglecting social and political action.

## Distinctions

### Advaita and Buddhism

Both traditions deny a permanent, separate self, and both emphasise liberation from suffering. But Advaita affirms a positive ultimate reality, pure consciousness, while Buddhism typically avoids positive metaphysical claims about an ultimate substance. Shankara criticised Buddhist views as nihilistic; Buddhist thinkers criticised Advaita as covert essentialism.

### Advaita and theistic Vedanta

Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita and Madhva's Dvaita reject strict non-dualism. They argue that the self and God are distinct, even if intimately related. The debate turns on how to read the Upanishads and on the coherence of identity between the finite and the infinite.

<Caveat title="Not pantheism">

Advaita is not simply the view that the universe is God. Brahman is not the sum of things; things derive their apparent existence from Brahman but do not exhaust it. The world is a manifestation under the conditions of ignorance, not a part of the divine essence.

</Caveat>

## Going deeper

### The problem of ignorance

If Brahman is the only reality, where does ignorance come from? Advaita's answer is that ignorance is beginningless and cannot be ultimately explained, because explanation belongs to the conventional level. This has struck critics as an evasion. The school replies that the question itself presupposes the duality it is meant to explain.

### The ethics of non-duality

If there is no ultimate other, what grounds the obligation to help others? Advaita can say that compassion arises naturally from the recognition of non-separation. But critics worry that the emphasis on inner realisation can distract from concrete injustice. The tension is not unique to Advaita; it appears in many mystical traditions.

## Living with the question

Advaita is not a denial of the world. It is an invitation to ask who the self really is. The practice is to notice the difference between the changing contents of experience and the awareness in which they appear.

That awareness is not a thing among things. It is the light by which things are known. Advaita's wager is that this light is not yours alone; it is the light of reality itself.

## What to carry away

Advaita Vedanta teaches that Atman is Brahman. Maya explains the appearance of plurality, and the two-truth doctrine preserves the reality of conventional life. Liberation is knowledge, not action. Whether the view is coherent is debated, but it remains one of the most rigorous non-dual philosophies in the world.
