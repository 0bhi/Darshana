Every day the sun rises. You expect it to rise tomorrow. You have seen thousands of times that bread nourishes and fire burns. You expect these regularities to continue. This expectation is so basic that it is rarely examined.

David Hume examined it, and the result was one of the most durable problems in philosophy.

## The problem

Inductive reasoning moves from observed cases to unobserved cases. It has the form:

1. All observed emeralds have been green.
2. Therefore, the next emerald will be green.

The conclusion is not logically guaranteed by the premises. It is possible that the next emerald is blue. The inference is probable at best, not deductive.

Hume asks what justifies the inference. We cannot justify it by experience, because experience only tells us about observed cases. We cannot justify it by reason alone, because reason alone cannot tell us what the future will be like.

## The uniformity of nature

One natural response is that induction is justified because nature is uniform. The future will resemble the past. But how do we know that? We know it because in the past the future has resembled the past. That is an inductive inference. The justification is circular.

Hume's conclusion is not that induction is irrational. It is that induction cannot be justified by reason or experience. We are caused by custom and habit to expect regularities. The belief is natural, but it is not rationally grounded in the way we might have hoped.

<ThoughtExperiment title="The new emerald">

Imagine that all emeralds observed before tomorrow are green. Now define "grue" as green if observed before tomorrow and blue otherwise.

All observed emeralds are green, so they are also grue. Should we predict that the next emerald is green or grue? Both predictions fit the evidence equally well. But they cannot both be right.

Nelson Goodman's paradox shows that the problem is not only about justifying induction; it is about specifying which generalizations are projectible.

</ThoughtExperiment>

## Responses

### Popper and falsification

Karl Popper accepts Hume's point and draws a radical conclusion: science does not use induction. Scientific method proceeds by conjecture and refutation. We propose hypotheses, derive predictions, and test them. A hypothesis that survives testing is not confirmed; it is merely not yet refuted.

This avoids the problem of induction but raises new questions. If theories are never confirmed, why should we prefer one to another? Popper appeals to empirical content, simplicity, and problem-solving power, but these criteria are not themselves derived from induction.

### Bayesian confirmation

Bayesian epistemologists treat induction as a form of probabilistic updating. We begin with prior probabilities and update them in light of evidence. The sun's rising a million times increases the probability that it will rise tomorrow, given suitable priors.

The problem is that the priors must come from somewhere. If they are arbitrary, the justification is incomplete. If they are derived from experience, the circularity returns.

### Pragmatic justification

Some philosophers argue that induction does not need a metaphysical justification. It is the only method we have for predicting the future, and it works. The pragmatic justification is not a proof; it is a defence of the practice.

### Ordinary language

P. F. Strawson argues that the demand for a justification of induction is confused. Induction is a standard of rational belief, not something that can be justified by a more fundamental standard. Asking for a justification of induction is like asking for a justification of deduction.

<Caveat title="The problem is not skepticism about science">

The problem of induction does not show that science is irrational. It shows that the rationality of science cannot be reduced to deductive proof. Scientific reasoning is a complex practice involving observation, experiment, modelling, and criticism. The problem asks us to be clear about what kind of justification that practice can have.

</Caveat>

## Going deeper

### The problem of the prior

Bayesianism is powerful, but it relocates the problem. Where do prior probabilities come from? If they are subjective, different agents can rationally reach different conclusions from the same evidence. If they are objective, we need an account of why.

### Induction and explanation

Some philosophers argue that we should prefer hypotheses that provide the best explanation of the evidence. Inference to the best explanation is a form of inductive reasoning, but it may be more tractable than simple enumeration. Whether it escapes Hume's problem is debated.

### The grue paradox

Goodman's paradox shows that the problem is not merely that induction is unjustified. It is that we need a theory of which predicates are projectible. "Green" is projectible; "grue" is not, even though the evidence supports both. The difference seems to lie in our practices and the way the predicates are entrenched, not in logic alone.

## Living with the question

The problem of induction is not a reason to stop expecting the sun to rise. It is a reason to be humble about the foundations of empirical knowledge. The regularities we rely on are not guaranteed by logic. They are supported by experience, habit, and the success of our practices.

## What to carry away

Hume's problem shows that inductive inferences cannot be justified without circularity. Popper, Bayesians, and pragmatists offer different responses, but none has achieved consensus. The problem remains a central challenge to empiricism and to the philosophy of science.
