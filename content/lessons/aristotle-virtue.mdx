Modern ethics often begins with a question: what should I do? Aristotle begins with a different question: what kind of person should I be? His answer is that the good life consists in flourishing, and flourishing consists in the exercise of virtue.

## Eudaimonia

Eudaimonia is often translated as happiness, but the translation is misleading. It is closer to flourishing or living well. It is not a feeling, though it includes pleasure. It is an activity, a way of living that realizes human capacities over a complete life.

Aristotle thinks everyone agrees that eudaimonia is the highest good. The disagreement is about what it consists in. Most people identify it with pleasure, wealth, or honour. Aristotle argues that these are incomplete. Pleasure is fleeting, wealth is a means, and honour depends on other people's opinions.

## The function argument

Aristotle's argument for the content of eudaimonia appeals to the human function. Everything has a characteristic activity, and the good of a thing is to perform that activity well.

1. The human function is rational activity.
2. The good of a human being is to perform the human function well.
3. Performing it well requires virtue.
4. Therefore, eudaimonia is activity of the soul in accordance with virtue.

The argument is controversial. Critics ask whether human beings have a single function, and whether the analogy with tools and organs is appropriate. But the general idea is powerful: a good human life is one that exercises distinctively human capacities well.

<ThoughtExperiment title="The happy pig">

John Stuart Mill famously asked whether it is better to be a dissatisfied human being than a satisfied pig. Aristotle's answer would be that the question is badly framed. The pig cannot flourish as a human can, because it lacks the capacities that make human flourishing possible.

A life of pleasure without the exercise of reason, friendship, and virtue may be pleasant, but it is not fully human.

</ThoughtExperiment>

## The doctrine of the mean

Aristotle defines virtue as a disposition to choose the mean between excess and deficiency, determined by reason and by the person of practical wisdom.

Courage, for example, is the mean between cowardice and recklessness. Generosity is the mean between stinginess and prodigality. The mean is not a mathematical average; it is relative to the situation and the person. What is courageous for a soldier may be reckless for a child.

This is why virtue cannot be reduced to rules. It requires *phronesis*, practical wisdom, the ability to perceive what a situation calls for and to act accordingly.

## Habituation

Virtues are acquired through practice. We become just by doing just acts, courageous by doing courageous acts. Aristotle compares moral education to learning a craft: repetition and guidance shape a disposition.

This does not mean that virtue is mere habit. The virtuous person acts knowingly, chooses the act for its own sake, and acts from a stable character. But the process begins with habituation, and character is formed by what we repeatedly do.

## Friendship and contemplation

Aristotle devotes considerable attention to friendship. Friends are necessary for a good life, not merely as instruments but as goods in themselves. The highest form of friendship is based on virtue: friends wish each other well for the other's sake.

In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, contemplation is presented as the highest activity, because it is the exercise of the highest human capacity, reason. This has puzzled readers who see it as elitist or otherworldly. Aristotle's point may be that a life of understanding is the most self-sufficient and most divine form of activity.

<Caveat title="Virtue is not a recipe">

The doctrine of the mean does not give a formula for every decision. It requires judgment, experience, and attention to context. This is a strength for some readers and a weakness for others.

</Caveat>

## Objections

### Vagueness

Critics argue that virtue ethics offers little guidance in difficult cases. If two virtues conflict, or if the mean is unclear, what should one do? Virtue ethicists respond that practical wisdom is precisely the capacity to navigate such cases, but this can seem circular.

### Relativism

If the mean is relative to the person and situation, does virtue ethics collapse into relativism? Aristotle believes that human nature and the human function provide an objective standard, but the standard is broad enough to allow cultural variation.

### Moral luck

Aristotle acknowledges that external goods - health, wealth, friends - are needed for flourishing. This means that a good life depends partly on factors beyond one's control. Critics argue that this makes eudaimonia vulnerable to luck in a way that undermines its ethical authority.

## Living with the question

Virtue ethics shifts attention from isolated decisions to patterns of character. It asks not only "What should I do?" but "What am I becoming?" The question is useful because it makes ethical life a project rather than a series of puzzles.

It also encourages attention to role models. We learn virtues by observing people who embody them, not only by reading rules.

## What to carry away

Aristotle's ethics centres on eudaimonia, the exercise of virtue, and practical wisdom. The doctrine of the mean provides a flexible guide to action, and habituation explains how character is formed. Virtue ethics is not a decision procedure, but it offers a rich account of what makes a life good.
